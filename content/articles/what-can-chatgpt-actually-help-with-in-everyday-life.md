Most of what is written about AI assumes you want to use it for work. Plenty of people do not. They want to know whether it is any use for the ordinary business of running a life.

It is. Here is where, based on what people actually come back and tell me they used it for.

## Understanding official letters

This is the one that surprises people most, and it is probably the most useful thing on the list.

Letters from HMRC, the council, a hospital, a pension provider, an insurer. Written in a register designed to protect the organisation rather than inform you. Paste the letter in and ask three things: what is this actually saying, what do I have to do, and by when.

You can go further. "Explain this as if I have never dealt with this before." "What are they not telling me?" "What should I ask when I ring them?"

For anyone helping an elderly parent with paperwork, this alone is worth the effort of learning it.

## Writing the message you have been putting off

The complaint to the builder. The note to the neighbour about the hedge. The email to the school. The polite but firm chaser to someone who owes you money.

These take a long time not because they are hard to write but because you keep second-guessing the tone. Say what you actually want to happen, ask for three versions with different levels of firmness, and pick the one that sounds like you. Ten minutes instead of an evening.

## Planning things with too many variables

Holidays, family gatherings, a garden, a house move, a party. Anything where you are holding too many constraints in your head at once.

Describe the constraints honestly. "Four adults, two children aged six and nine, one who does not walk far, a week in September, budget around this much, we want a mix of proper rest and one or two days out." You will get a plan you can argue with, which is far more useful than a blank page.

## The daily food question

Not a glamorous use, but a real one. List what is actually in your fridge and cupboards and ask for five meals plus a shopping list for the gaps. Add your constraints: no dairy, twenty minutes maximum, a teenager who will not eat anything green.

People underestimate how much mental load this removes.

## Learning something without a course

This is where these tools are genuinely good, and underused.

You can ask about anything, at any level, as many times as you want, without the small social cost of admitting you do not understand. "Explain compound interest as if I know nothing." "Right, I followed that, but why does the second part matter?" "Give me an example with real numbers."

No tutor has that patience. Nobody is sighing at you.

## Making sense of things written for experts

Insurance policies. Terms and conditions. A medical leaflet. A legal letter. A technical manual for something you have just bought.

Paste it in and ask for the plain version, then ask what you should be paying attention to. On anything medical or legal, use it to understand the shape of the thing and then check the specifics with a professional. It is a translator, not an adviser.

## Family and household admin

Helping with a personal statement. Working out what to write in a difficult condolence card. Drafting a letter of complaint that will actually get read. Preparing questions before an appointment so you do not walk out having forgotten the important one.

## Two rules for the ordinary stuff

**Do not trust the specifics.** Dates, prices, dosages, legal deadlines, phone numbers. It will produce these with total confidence and it is sometimes wrong. Check anything that matters against the original source.

**Do not paste in what you would not want on a screen behind you.** Bank details, medical records, other people's private information, your national insurance number. Take the identifying details out first. The information is usually still useful without them.

## Where to begin

Take the next letter that lands on your doormat and confuses you. Paste it in. Ask what it means.

That one exchange tends to do more to remove the fear than any amount of explanation, because you get something immediately useful out of it and nothing about it required you to be technical.
