Somebody in your feed has a list. Fifty AI tools you need in 2026. Twenty that will change how you work. Ten your competitors are already using.

You save the post. You don't open it. You feel slightly worse than before you saw it.

Let me take that off your plate.

## Tool lists are content, not advice

Those lists exist because they get shared. They're written to make you feel late. They're rarely written by someone who has used all fifty tools in real work, because nobody has.

Meanwhile, most of the actual value available to you right now sits inside one or two general assistants that you probably already have access to. The specialist tools are mostly a nicer wrapper around the same underlying capability, with a subscription attached.

## Depth beats breadth, by a long way

Here's what I see in workshops. Someone who has used one tool properly for six months runs rings around someone who has dabbled in fifteen. Not because their tool is better. Because they know how it behaves.

They know when it drifts off. They know how to pull it back. They've built up a set of briefs that work for their job. They know what it's bad at, so they stop asking. That knowledge only comes from repetition on real work.

Switching tools every few weeks resets you to zero each time. You stay permanently at the beginner stage, which is exactly the stage that feels disappointing.

## The tools will change anyway

Whatever is best today will be second-best within a year. Companies get bought, features move, prices go up, the popular one becomes the expensive one.

If you've built your competence around one product, that's fragile. If you've built it around understanding how these systems behave, how to brief them, where they fail, what needs checking, it moves with you. You'll pick up the next tool in an afternoon.

That's the difference between learning software and learning a skill.

## What to actually do

Pick one general assistant. Any of the main ones is fine, they're close enough in ability that the choice matters less than people pretend.

Use it for three months on real work only. No experiments, no playing, no "let me see if it can write a poem." Actual tasks from your actual week.

Keep a note of the briefs that work. When you find a way of asking that gets you a good result, save it somewhere. That file becomes surprisingly valuable.

Add a second tool only when you hit a wall the first one genuinely can't handle. Images, maybe. Transcription. Something specific, driven by a real need rather than a headline.

## The permission bit

You are allowed to ignore most of what's being posted about AI. The urgency in that content is manufactured, and it's aimed at people who feel uncertain enough to click.

You're not going to be left behind because you didn't try the tool that was popular for three weeks in March. You'd be left behind by not building the underlying skill at all, and that takes one tool and a few months of ordinary use.

Slow, narrow and consistent wins this. It usually does.
