You opened ChatGPT. You typed something like "write me a LinkedIn post about leadership." You got back four paragraphs of soft, agreeable nothing. You closed the tab and thought, right, so that's what everyone's excited about.

Fair reaction. But here's what actually happened.

## You gave it nothing to work with

Think about how you'd brief a capable new starter on their first morning. You wouldn't say "write me a post about leadership" and walk off. You'd tell them who it's for, what point you're making, what you sound like, how long it should be, and what a good one looks like.

AI tools respond to briefing quality more than anything else. Give it three words, you get three words' worth of thinking back. Give it the context you'd give a person, and the quality changes immediately.

Try the difference yourself:

**Weak:** Write a LinkedIn post about leadership.

**Better:** I'm an operations director with 18 years in manufacturing. Write a 150-word LinkedIn post for other operations leaders about why most "open door policies" fail. My angle: people don't avoid your door, they avoid the conversation that happens after. Plain language, no corporate speak, no hashtags, first person.

Same tool. Completely different output. Nothing changed except the brief.

## You asked it to do the wrong job

The other common mistake is asking it to be the expert. It isn't. You are.

Where these tools genuinely earn their keep is on the work around the thinking, not the thinking itself. Turning your messy notes into a clean first draft. Summarising a 40-page report so you know which ten pages to actually read. Rewriting the same message for four different audiences. Pulling questions out of a transcript. Checking your document for the thing you always forget.

Ask it to have the idea and you'll get something generic, because generic is the average of everything it's read. Bring the idea yourself and ask it to do the work around the idea, and it's fast and genuinely useful.

## You expected it to be right

This one catches experienced people out, because we're used to software being accurate. A calculator doesn't guess. This does.

These tools produce the most plausible-sounding next thing. Plausible and correct overlap most of the time, which is exactly what makes the gaps dangerous. Anything with a number, a name, a date, a legal point or a citation needs checking before it leaves your hands.

That's not a reason to avoid it. It's a reason to use it where being 90% right and fast is genuinely useful, and to slow down where being wrong costs you.

## A better first hour

If your first attempt put you off, do this instead. Pick one task you did last week that you didn't enjoy and that involved words. A summary, a proposal, a set of meeting notes, a difficult email you rewrote five times.

Open the tool. Paste in the real material. Tell it who you are, who the output is for, and what a good version looks like. Then talk to it. Say "too formal." Say "shorter." Say "you've missed the point about the budget." It will adjust.

Twenty minutes on one real task will teach you more than an hour of tutorials on someone else's example.

## What this actually is

You have decades of judgement about what good looks like in your field. That's the hard part, and it took you years. What you're adding is a fast, tireless assistant who writes quickly, never gets bored, and needs supervision.

You already know how to brief people and check work. That's the skill. You're just applying it to something new.
