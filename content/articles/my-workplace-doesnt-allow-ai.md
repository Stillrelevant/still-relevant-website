I ran a workshop recently and put out a flyer with the word AI on it. Several people told me afterwards they had nearly not come, because their employer does not allow AI at work, so they assumed the session had nothing for them.

That reaction taught me something. When most people hear AI, they picture using it *on the job*, inside company systems, with company data. And if that door is closed, the whole subject looks closed.

It is not. Here is what sits on the other side of that assumption.

## The difference between using it at work and using it for work

Your employer's policy is about company information going into an outside system. That is a reasonable thing to control and you should absolutely respect it.

But almost nothing on the list below involves company information. It happens on your own time, on your own device, and it makes you better at your job without a single confidential detail leaving the building.

## Preparing for the meeting you are dreading

You have a meeting on Thursday where you know somebody is going to push back.

At home, in general terms, you can talk it through. "I need to make the case for hiring one more person on a team that has already had its budget questioned. What are the six objections I will face? Which is the strongest? What is the weakest part of my own argument?"

No names, no figures, no company detail. Just you, thinking out loud with something that answers back. You walk in on Thursday having already had the argument once.

## Getting your head around something complicated

Every job has a bit you nod along to and do not fully follow. The financial model. The regulation. The technical explanation from another department. The bit of jargon everyone else seems comfortable with.

You can ask about all of it, in general terms, as many times as you need, without the small social cost of admitting you did not understand in the meeting. "Explain this as if I know nothing. Now explain it again with an example. Now what would somebody get wrong about it?"

That is not a workaround. That is just learning, and it is the single most underrated thing these tools do.

## Practising

Interviews. Presentations. Difficult conversations. A pitch. The awkward chat with someone who reports to you.

You can rehearse as many times as you like, ask to be pushed harder, and get told afterwards which of your answers were vague. Nobody sighs at you. Nobody gets bored on the fourth run-through.

## Thinking through a career decision

Should you go for the promotion. Should you retrain. Should you stay another year. Whether the thing you have been circling for eighteen months is actually a good idea.

Lay it out and ask for the strongest argument against your instinct. It will not decide for you and you should not let it. But it will get you past the same three thoughts you have been having on the drive home.

## Improving how you write

Not writing for you. Improving what you already wrote.

Draft the thing yourself, then ask what is unclear, what is too long, and what a reader might misunderstand. You keep your voice and your judgement. You just get an editor who is always available.

## Exploring an idea before you commit

A side project. A business idea. A qualification. A change of direction. Anything where you would like to understand the shape of it before you spend real money or real months on it.

## The rules that keep you safe

Since the whole point here is respecting your workplace policy, be clear about it:

- **Do not paste in anything confidential.** Client names, staff details, unpublished figures, internal documents. Not a redacted version, not "just this once."
- **Keep it general.** "A manufacturing business with a supply chain problem" rather than your employer and your supplier.
- **Use your own device and your own time.** That is the whole distinction.
- **If you are not sure, ask.** Most policies are about data leaving the organisation. Reading yours properly is worth twenty minutes.

## The honest summary

Your employer's policy controls what goes into the tool. It does not control what you understand, how well prepared you walk into a room, or what you are able to learn on a Sunday afternoon.

Those are the things that actually shape a career. And none of them need permission.
