If you run a small business, you already know the problem. It is not that you lack ideas. It is that you are the only one who can act on them, and the day fills up with the same handful of jobs that have to be done and nobody else will do.

So the useful question is not "what can AI do." It is "which of the jobs that eat my week can I stop doing personally, without the quality dropping."

Here is where it actually lands.

## Enquiries and follow-up

Most small businesses lose money in the gap between someone being interested and someone getting a reply. Not because you are careless, but because the reply lands on your list at nine at night.

Build a set of ready answers for the enquiries you get over and over. Not templates you paste blindly, but strong starting points you adjust in thirty seconds. Pricing questions, availability, what happens next, why you cost more than the other quote.

The same applies to follow-up, which almost everybody neglects. A short, specific follow-up written from your notes of the call takes two minutes instead of twenty, so it actually gets sent.

## Proposals and quotes

This is where the hours go for consultants, trades, agencies and anyone selling bespoke work.

Feed in your notes from the conversation, your standard terms and your pricing structure, and ask for a first draft aimed at that specific client. You will rewrite parts of it. That is fine. Rewriting takes a fraction of the time of starting from nothing, and the version you send is more tailored than the one you would have produced by copying last month's.

## Marketing that does not stop after three weeks

Every small business owner has the same content story. A burst of enthusiasm, two good weeks, then silence for a month.

The realistic fix is to stop generating content from scratch and start harvesting it. Record yourself for five minutes answering a question a customer actually asked. Get the transcript. Ask for a short post, a longer piece, three captions and an email from that one recording.

It works because the raw material is genuinely yours. The tone stays right because the words started as your words. Content written from a blank prompt sounds like everybody else's, and customers can tell.

## Understanding your own numbers

Most owners have data they never look at properly, because opening the spreadsheet on a Friday afternoon is nobody's idea of a good time.

Export the figures with the customer names removed and ask specific questions. Which months are consistently weak. Where the margin actually sits by service line. What changed compared with last year and what might explain it.

You still make the decisions. What you get is a faster route to the questions worth asking.

## Admin that has no strategic value at all

Job adverts. Supplier emails. Meeting notes into actions. Policy documents you are required to have and nobody will read. Turning a scribbled list into an invoice description that makes sense to the client.

None of this grows the business. All of it takes time. Hand it over.

## What to keep well away

**Anything with a client's confidential information in it**, unless you have checked where that data goes and your contracts allow it. This catches people out constantly. Strip the names.

**Anything you would sign.** Contracts, terms, anything with legal or tax consequence. Draft with it, then pay a professional to check. That is still cheaper than the alternative.

**Your actual pricing decisions.** It will happily produce a confident recommendation based on nothing about your market.

**The relationship work.** The apology when you have let someone down. The difficult conversation with a supplier. The thank you to a client who has stayed with you for years. Write those yourself, badly if necessary. People can tell.

## Being honest about the cost

The first three attempts at anything will take longer than doing it yourself. Briefing properly is work. You will go back and forth and think you could have written it by now.

That is the price of the first few goes. By the fourth, you have a brief that works and it takes ninety seconds. The owners getting real value are not doing anything clever. They pushed through the awkward stage on three or four jobs they repeat every single week.

Pick your three. Not thirty. The ones that come round every week, that you find tedious, and that involve words. Get those genuinely good before you add anything else.
