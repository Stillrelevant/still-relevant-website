There is a story going round that experience is now a liability. That the person with twenty years in the job is at a disadvantage against someone in their twenties who grew up with this technology.

I have taught enough rooms of both to tell you it is the wrong way round, and here is why.

## What these tools are bad at is what you are good at

AI produces fluent, confident, plausible output. Endlessly, instantly, on any subject. What it cannot do is tell you whether the output is any good in your context.

It does not know that this client always says yes in the meeting and changes their mind on Thursday. It does not know that the last time your organisation tried this, it failed for reasons nobody wrote down. It does not know which of the five recommendations will not survive contact with your finance director.

You know all of that. It took you years and it is not written down anywhere, which is precisely why no system has learned it.

When output becomes cheap and abundant, judgement becomes the scarce half. That is the half you are holding.

## Where the real risk sits

Being honest, though: there is a risk. It is just not the one people talk about.

The risk is not that you are too old. It is that a big part of many experienced roles is the work *around* the expertise. Writing things up. Summarising. Formatting. Reporting. Chasing. Producing the document that proves the thinking happened.

That layer is thinning fast. If your value is mostly in that layer, that is worth taking seriously now rather than in two years.

The response is not to defend the layer. It is to move your visible value up into the judgement, and to use the tools to handle what is underneath so you have room to.

## Four moves that actually shift things

**Get fluent on your own work, not on demos.** Twenty minutes a day for a fortnight on real tasks from your real week will put you ahead of most of your colleagues. Not because you learned something clever, but because almost nobody does the boring repetition.

**Become the person who can tell good from bad.** As AI-assisted work floods into your organisation, the ability to review it properly is going to be worth a lot. Get good at spotting the confident wrong answer. Say so in meetings. That is a visible, valuable skill and very few people have it yet.

**Speak up about the risks, without being the person who blocks everything.** Data, confidentiality, accuracy, the difference between drafting and deciding. Experienced people are well placed to hold this line, and organisations badly need someone who can do it without either panic or naivety.

**Teach one person.** Nothing establishes your position faster than being the one who explained it to a colleague without making them feel stupid. It also forces you to understand it properly.

## What to stop doing

Stop saying "I'm not technical" out loud. It is rarely accurate, and every time you say it you make it slightly more true in other people's minds and your own.

Stop apologising for asking basic questions. In every session I run, the "basic" question turns out to be the one half the room wanted to ask.

Stop trying to keep up with the news cycle around this. It is designed to make you feel behind. The underlying skill moves far more slowly than the headlines suggest.

## The honest version

Nobody can promise you your role will look the same in five years. It probably will not, and that would be true with or without any of this.

What I can tell you, having watched a lot of people go through it, is that the ones who came out well were not the most technical. They were the ones who stayed curious, put in a few unglamorous hours on real work, and refused to accept the story that they were past it.

The experience you are worried about is the asset. It just needs today's tools behind it.
