Let me take one thing off the table straight away. You do not need to understand how these systems work in order to use them well.

You almost certainly do not know how your car's engine management system works either, and you drive perfectly well. You know what the car is for, what it is bad at, when to slow down and when something sounds wrong. That is exactly the level of understanding you need here.

## Start with what you already do best

The people who describe themselves as "not technical" usually have an enormous advantage they have not noticed. You know what good work looks like in your field. You can read a report and tell within a page whether the person understood the subject. You can spot a proposal written by someone who did not listen on the call.

That skill is the whole game. AI produces plausible output at speed. Somebody has to say "this bit is fine, this bit is wrong, this bit misses the point entirely." If you can do that, you can use these tools better than a technically confident twenty-five-year-old who cannot.

## The one habit that changes everything

Almost every disappointing result comes from the same thing: a one-line request.

Think of it as briefing a very fast, very literal new starter who has read a great deal and knows nothing about your organisation. You would never say "write the client update" and walk away. You would tell them who the client is, what happened this month, what you are worried they will ask, and how formal to be.

So do that. A useful brief has four parts:

**Who you are.** "I am a practice manager at a six-partner GP surgery."

**Who it is for.** "This is for our patient participation group, mostly retired, not medical."

**What you want.** "A 400-word update explaining the new appointment system, why we changed it, and what to do if they cannot get online."

**What good looks like.** "Warm but not chatty. No NHS jargon. Do not apologise repeatedly."

That takes ninety seconds to type and it is the difference between something usable and something bland.

## Where it actually helps in a working week

Not everywhere. Here is where I see real gains for non-technical roles:

- **The first draft of anything.** Reports, updates, agendas, policies, job adverts, that email you have rewritten four times.
- **Reading things you would otherwise skim.** Paste in a long document and ask what it says, what it wants from you and what the deadline is.
- **Changing the register.** The same message for your board, your team and your customers, without writing it three times.
- **Getting unstuck.** "I need to tell my team about a budget cut. What are the four things they will worry about?"
- **Being the awkward colleague.** "Read this as a sceptical finance director. Where would you push back?"

Notice that none of these ask the tool to know your job. They ask it to do the writing and sorting around your job.

## What to be careful with

**Confidential information.** Client names, staff records, patient details, unpublished figures. Find out what your organisation allows before you paste anything in, not after. If in doubt, take the names out.

**Anything you would sign.** Legal wording, medical detail, financial figures, regulatory language. Use it to draft, then check every specific yourself.

**Facts that sound right.** It will give you a wrong date in exactly the same confident tone as a right one. Anything with a number, a name or a citation gets verified.

## A realistic first fortnight

Week one: pick one task you do every week that involves words and that you find tedious. Just one. Do it with the tool three times, briefing properly each time. Notice what works and save the brief that got you the best result.

Week two: add a second task. Start a simple note on your desktop called "briefs that work" and paste the good ones in. That file will become the most valuable thing you build.

That is it. No course required, no new vocabulary, no becoming technical. Two tasks, done properly, will teach you more than a month of reading about it.

## The part nobody says out loud

The reason this feels intimidating is not the technology. It is the fear of looking foolish in front of people who seem to already know.

Almost nobody already knows. The confident ones are mostly further along the same fumbling path you are about to start. There is no exam, nobody is marking you, and the only real risk is deciding it is not for you before you have given it one honest fortnight.

You have handled bigger changes than this. This one just has a lot of noise around it.
